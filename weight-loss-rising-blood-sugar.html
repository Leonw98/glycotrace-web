Losing Weight but Blood Sugar Rising? Why That Combination Is Worth Checking

By Leon Wilkinson, Type 3c patient and developer of GlycoTrace · Published August 2026

I write this as a patient, not a doctor. This page exists to help you have a better conversation with your own clinician, not to replace one, and definitely not to help you diagnose yourself.

Quick answer

Losing weight normally makes blood sugar better. So when someone loses weight and their glucose readings get worse at the same time, the two are moving in a direction that does not usually go together, and that is worth mentioning to a doctor.

Most of the time there is an ordinary explanation. Occasionally the pattern is the first visible sign that something is wrong with the pancreas itself. Either way, it is a reason for a conversation, not a reason to panic.

This article is part of our complete guide to Type 3c diabetes, which covers what it is, its causes, diagnosis and day-to-day management.

Why the combination is unusual

For most people living with Type 2 diabetes, losing excess weight is one of the most reliable things that improves blood sugar. Insulin works better, readings come down, and the numbers on the meter follow the numbers on the scales in the same direction. It is the one bit of the whole business that behaves predictably.

Which is why the opposite is so striking. If someone is losing weight and their glucose is climbing at the same time, the usual mechanism has been overridden by something else. The weight loss should be helping. Something is out-shouting it.

Doctors have a name for this general idea: a finding that does not fit the expected pattern. It does not tell you what is wrong. It tells you that the current explanation is incomplete.

A published case that shows the pattern clearly

In 2023, two clinicians at the Joslin Diabetes Center in Boston published a case report in the American Diabetes Association's journal Clinical Diabetes describing exactly this situation.

Their patient was a 56-year-old woman, a recreational cyclist, whose Type 2 diabetes had previously gone into remission. She lost around two stone (28 pounds, about 12.7 kg). Ordinarily that would be a success story. Instead her blood sugar went the wrong way: her HbA1c climbed from 5.0% up to 7%, and her glucose reached levels around 17.8 mmol/L (321 mg/dL). She also had abdominal pain.

One detail from the case is worth pausing on. A body composition scan showed that the weight she had lost was entirely lean mass, not fat. That is a different kind of weight loss from the sort that comes with dieting or more cycling, and it is part of what made the picture concerning.

Her diagnosis, in the end, was pancreatic cancer.

Important context before you read on

That is one case report about one person. A single case cannot tell you how likely anything is, and it is published precisely because it is unusual and instructive, not because it is typical.

If you have lost weight and your readings have gone up, the overwhelmingly likely explanations are ordinary ones. Please do not read this page and conclude anything about yourself. Read it, then tell your GP what you have noticed, and let them do the thinking.

The numbers, and what they actually mean

The case report cites two figures worth understanding properly, because both are easy to misread.

Around 1% of people aged 50 or over who newly meet the criteria for diabetes are diagnosed with pancreatic cancer within three years. Read that the other way round, which is the more useful way: roughly 99 out of every 100 are not. This is not a common outcome. It is, however, common enough that clinicians are taught to keep it in mind when a new diabetes diagnosis in an older adult looks atypical.

Up to about 75% of people with pancreatic cancer have raised blood sugar. This one runs in the opposite direction and is easy to get backwards. It says most people with the cancer have high glucose. It says nothing at all about how many people with high glucose have the cancer. The vast majority do not.

The reason both numbers matter is the timing. In some cases the diabetes appears before the cancer is found, which means a new and strangely behaved diabetes diagnosis is occasionally the earliest visible sign of something happening in the pancreas.

The much more likely explanations

Before anyone's mind goes to the worst place, there is a long list of far more common reasons that glucose might climb while weight falls:

  • An infection or any acute illness, which pushes glucose up temporarily
  • Steroids, and a number of other common medications
  • Significant stress, poor sleep, or a period of pain
  • An overactive thyroid, which can cause weight loss and affect glucose
  • Diabetes progressing naturally, where the pancreas gradually makes less insulin over time
  • Being the wrong type of diabetes all along, which is more common than most people realise

That last one is worth its own section.

When the label itself is the problem

Not all diabetes is Type 1 or Type 2. Type 3c diabetes, also called pancreatogenic diabetes, is caused by damage to the pancreas itself: pancreatitis, pancreatic surgery, cystic fibrosis, or a tumour.

The case report makes a point that matters here. When the pancreas is damaged broadly, it is not only insulin that falls. Glucagon and pancreatic polypeptide drop too. That is why Type 3c genuinely behaves differently from Type 2 rather than just being a variation of it, and why treatment that assumes Type 2 can fit badly.

It is also very frequently recorded as Type 2 in the notes. I have Type 3c myself, following pancreatitis, and I was initially coded as Type 2. That experience is common enough that it is a recognised problem in UK primary care data, and it is the reason this site exists at all.

So if your diabetes has never quite behaved the way the leaflets describe, that is worth saying out loud to your clinician. The differences between Type 3c and Type 2 are covered in more detail elsewhere on this site.

What to do if this sounds like you

Something specific and unalarming:

  • Book an appointment with your GP or diabetes team. A routine one is fine.
  • Describe the pattern, not just the symptom. Say roughly how much weight you have lost, over what period, and crucially whether you were trying to. Unintentional weight loss is the part clinicians want to hear about.
  • Bring your readings. Weeks or months of glucose data, next to your weight over the same period, says something no single appointment-day reading can.
  • Mention abdominal pain if you have it. In the published case, pain alongside worsening diabetes was one of the things that prompted a closer look.
  • Do not change or stop any medication or insulin on your own. Nothing on this page is a reason to alter your treatment.

The authors of the case report put their own recommendation in plain terms: worsening blood glucose despite significant weight loss should prompt an assessment for secondary causes, and abdominal pain alongside new or worsening diabetes should trigger prompt assessment. That is a message aimed at clinicians. Your job is only to give them the information that lets them act on it.

Why this is really an argument for keeping records

The thing that made this case legible was not any single measurement. A glucose reading of 17.8 mmol/L on its own is just a high reading with a dozen possible causes. Two stone of weight loss on its own might be good news.

What mattered was the two of them together, over time, moving in opposite directions. That is a pattern, and patterns that unfold over months are almost impossible to reconstruct from memory in a ten-minute appointment. You end up saying "I think it's been a bit high lately", which is true and useless.

This is the honest case for logging, and it is not really about apps. Whether you use a notebook, a spreadsheet, the app I built, or anything else, the value is the same: when something does not fit, you have the evidence to show someone who can act on it. GlycoTrace keeps glucose, food, insulin and enzyme doses on one timeline for exactly this reason, but the principle stands whatever you use.

The one thing to take away

Weight loss and blood sugar normally move in the same direction. When they do not, that is information. It is not a diagnosis, it is not a reason to be frightened, and it is very unlikely to be the worst case.

It is simply a good reason to tell your doctor what you have noticed, and to bring the numbers with you.
